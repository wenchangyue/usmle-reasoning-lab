quick_answer: >-
  Mismatch-repair–deficient (**dMMR**, usually **MSI-high**) tumors cannot correct DNA polymerase errors, so mutations
  pile up, especially **frameshifts** at microsatellite repeats. That creates many more **mutation-derived
  neoantigens**. Peptides made inside the tumor cell are displayed on **MHC class I** (proteasome → TAP → loading in
  the ER with β2-microglobulin) and recognized by **CD8+ T cells**. Anti-PD-1 antibodies such as **pembrolizumab** only

faq:
  - q: What is the difference between MHC class I and MHC class II?
    a: >-
      MHC class I is on almost all nucleated cells and presents peptides from proteins made inside the cell (self,
      viral, mutant tumor proteins) after the proteasome → TAP → ER route. It is one heavy chain (α1–α3) plus
      β2-microglobulin, has a closed groove holding about 8–10 amino acids, and is read by CD8+ T cells, which can kill
      the cell. MHC class II is expressed mainly by professional antigen-presenting cells (dendritic cells,
      macrophages, B cells) and presents peptides from swallowed material processed in endosomes and lysosomes. The
      invariant chain holds its groove until HLA-DM swaps in a peptide. It is an α plus a β chain with an open groove
      (about 13–25 amino acids) and is read by CD4+ helper T cells. Genes: class I HLA-A, -B, -C; class II HLA-DP,
      -DQ, -DR. Memory aid: MHC I × CD8 = 8; MHC II × CD4 = 8.
  - q: Why does loss of MSH2 usually come with loss of MSH6 on immunohistochemistry?
    a: >-
      The mismatch repair proteins work as pairs, and MSH6 is unstable without its partner MSH2; likewise PMS2 is
      unstable without MLH1. So MSH2 loss usually shows MSH2 + MSH6 loss, and MLH1 loss usually shows MLH1 + PMS2 loss.
      Isolated MSH6 or PMS2 loss can also occur.
  - q: Why is biallelic loss of beta-2-microglobulin not the answer?
    a: >-
      Without β2-microglobulin, classical MHC class I largely fails to reach the cell surface, so direct recognition by
      CD8+ T cells weakens. That removes display; it adds no target, so it cannot explain a better response. It is a
      recognized immune-escape route, but it does not guarantee that checkpoint blockade will fail; some B2M-deficient
      colorectal cancers still benefit.
  - q: How do you tell Lynch syndrome from sporadic mismatch-repair deficiency?
    a: >-
      Lynch syndrome is an autosomal dominant germline mutation in MLH1, MSH2, MSH6 or PMS2 (or a 3′ EPCAM deletion that
      silences MSH2), with a second hit in the tumor. Sporadic dMMR usually comes from MLH1 promoter hypermethylation,
      which shows MLH1 + PMS2 loss and often BRAF V600E. In colorectal cancer with MLH1 loss, BRAF V600E strongly favors
      a sporadic origin, but not absolutely. Both routes lead to the same immunological mechanism.
  - q: What does a checkpoint inhibitor actually do?
    a: >-
      PD-1 is an inhibitory receptor on activated T cells; PD-L1 on tumor or immune cells binds it and suppresses the
      T cell. Interferon-γ released by recognizing T cells can raise tumor PD-L1 (adaptive resistance). Pembrolizumab
      and nivolumab are IgG4 anti-PD-1 antibodies that work by blocking, not by recruiting Fc-mediated killing.
      Ipilimumab blocks a different brake, CTLA-4. A checkpoint inhibitor releases a brake; it does not supply a
      target.
