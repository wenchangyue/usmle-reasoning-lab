quick_answer: >-
  Blocking IP3 from binding its intracellular receptor, then stimulating with phenylephrine,
  lowers the activity of **protein kinase C**. Phenylephrine is a selective **α1** agonist; α1
  couples to **Gq**; Gq activates **PLC-β**, which cuts **PIP2** into **IP3** and **DAG**.
  Blocking the IP3 receptor stops calcium release but leaves DAG untouched — and **conventional
  PKC requires both DAG and calcium**, so it is the only enzyme that loses a required input.
  **Phospholipase C is upstream of the block** and keeps cutting PIP2; adenylate cyclase and
  phosphodiesterase belong to the cAMP arm, which α1 never engages; lipoxygenase sits on the
  phospholipase A2 line. (In a real cell calcium does feed back on PLC, so PLC can dip slightly —
  but the stem asks which enzyme is *most likely* to fall, and that is PKC.)
faq:
  - q: What exactly IS Gq — a subunit, or a separate protein?
    a: >-
      Gq is a complete, separate protein sitting on the inner face of the membrane, made of three
      subunits: α, β and γ. It is not part of the receptor and not a domain of it. The receptor is a
      different molecule that grabs it and lets it go. "Gq" specifically means the trimer whose alpha
      subunit is the q type.
  - q: Are Gs, Gi and Gq three different proteins, or the same protein with different receptors?
    a: >-
      Same chassis, different engine. All three are α + βγ trimers, and the βγ half is effectively
      shared. What differs is the **alpha subunit** — αs, αi or αq. The little letter names the alpha,
      never the receptor. Because alpha is the piece that detaches and goes to find an enzyme, the
      alpha type alone decides what happens downstream.
  - q: What does the G protein actually DO? Does it hold the receptor in place?
    a: >-
      No — it has no structural job at all. It is a switch with a built-in timer. Alpha holds GDP when
      off; the activated receptor pries that pocket open, GTP replaces GDP, the trimer comes apart, and
      free alpha-GTP switches an enzyme on. Then alpha hydrolyses its own GTP back to GDP and re-docks.
      Nothing external turns it off; every switch is born with a fuse already lit.
  - q: Why does GTP replace GDP, and not another GDP?
    a: >-
      Nothing chooses it. The nucleotide pocket is specific for guanine nucleotides — ATP never gets in
      — but between GDP and GTP it does not discriminate. The cell simply keeps far more GTP around than
      GDP (textbooks put the ratio near ten to one), so an open pocket in a room full of GTP refills with
      GTP. No sensor, no proofreading, no energy spent.
  - q: Which subunit is the actor, and what does beta-gamma do?
    a: >-
      Alpha is the executive: it holds the nucleotide and it is the piece that leaves and touches the
      effector. Beta and gamma bind each other so tightly they behave as one part, and at exam level you
      can treat βγ as what holds alpha in the off position. It is not inert, though — in the SA node,
      M2 → Gi releases βγ, which opens GIRK potassium channels and slows the heart alongside the fall in
      cAMP.
  - q: Why do some G proteins stimulate and others inhibit?
    a: >-
      Because αs and αi go to the *same* enzyme, adenylate cyclase, and give it opposite instructions.
      The letters are the answer: s for stimulatory, i for inhibitory. You do not have to memorise what
      they do — you read it. Only q carries no hint, which makes it the single item in this system you
      genuinely have to learn.
  - q: Why does a receptor need a G protein at all?
    a: >-
      Because of what seven transmembrane passes cost it. The receptor is stitched into the membrane, so
      although its shape change genuinely does carry information from the outer face to the inner face,
      that is the end of what it can do alone — it cannot leave, cannot be in two places, and cannot
      multiply. So it hands off to something that can let go. Note the limit: the released subunits stay
      lipid-tethered and slide **along the inner face** of the membrane; they are not couriers into the
      cytosol.
  - q: Isn't the alpha-1 receptor's "alpha" the same as the G protein's alpha subunit?
    a: >-
      No, and this collision quietly wrecks people's understanding for years. The alpha in "α1 receptor"
      classifies adrenergic receptors (alpha type versus beta type) and describes what binds on the
      outside. The alpha subunit is a physical piece of a different protein on the inside. Rule of thumb:
      letters with numbers after them (α1, α2, β1, β2) are receptors; αs, αi, αq and βγ are G protein parts.
  - q: How do I actually remember this pathway instead of losing it every week?
    a: >-
      Stop memorising arrows and derive the geography. PIP2's head carries phosphates, so it is charged,
      so it must leave the membrane — that is IP3, and its target therefore has to be somewhere else (it
      is on the ER). The tail is two fatty acids, so it is greasy and cannot leave — that is DAG, so
      whatever uses DAG has to come to the membrane, which is why PKC relocates. Then say five sentences
      out loud from the single word "phenylephrine". Rebuilding beats re-reading.
  - q: Which receptors go with which G protein, and how much must I memorise?
    a: >-
      Only eight items, and only within the standard autonomic set the exam asks about. **Gq: H1, α1, V1,
      M1, M3** ("HAVe 1 M&M"). **Gi: α2, M2, D2.** Everything else on that list — β1, β2, β3, D1, H2, V2 —
      is Gs. That mapping is an evolutionary accident with no derivable logic, which is exactly why it is
      the only part worth rote learning. There is also a fourth family, G12/13, not covered here.
