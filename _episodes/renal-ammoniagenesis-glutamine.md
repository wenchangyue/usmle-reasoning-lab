faq:
  - q: Which amino acid do the kidneys use to increase acid excretion during metabolic acidosis?
    a: >-
      Glutamine. Proximal tubule cells metabolize it through phosphate-dependent glutaminase
      and glutamate dehydrogenase. The pathway supplies ammonium for urinary excretion and
      a carbon skeleton whose metabolism generates new bicarbonate for the blood.
  - q: Does glutamine directly neutralize acid in the blood?
    a: >-
      No. In this question glutamine is a metabolic substrate, not an antacid drug. The
      kidney takes it up and metabolizes it. Ammonium excretion and bicarbonate generation
      are the resulting physiological processes; the lesson does not recommend glutamine
      supplementation as treatment for acidosis.
  - q: Why does one glutamine molecule supply two ammonium ions?
    a: >-
      Glutamine contains a side-chain amide nitrogen and an alpha-amino nitrogen. Glutaminase
      releases the first nitrogen as ammonium while making glutamate. Glutamate dehydrogenase
      releases the second while making alpha-ketoglutarate. The familiar two ammonium and
      two bicarbonate result summarizes the net pathway, not one balanced chemical reaction.
  - q: Is newly generated bicarbonate different from reabsorbed bicarbonate?
    a: >-
      Yes. Reabsorbing filtered bicarbonate prevents loss of base already present in the body.
      New bicarbonate generation adds base. For renal ammonia metabolism to provide the
      corresponding net acid-base benefit, the ammonia must be excreted in urine; return
      to blood and hepatic nitrogen disposal can offset that benefit.
  - q: Does acidic urine prove that enough acid is being excreted?
    a: >-
      No. Urine pH reflects free hydrogen ion activity, not the total amount of acid leaving
      the body. Most urinary acid is carried by buffers, including ammonium and phosphate.
      Net acid excretion depends on ammonium plus titratable acid excretion minus urinary
      bicarbonate loss, so urine pH alone is insufficient.
  - q: Why is aspartate not the answer, and when would it be correct?
    a: >-
      Aspartate supplies a nitrogen for hepatic urea synthesis at the argininosuccinate
      synthetase step, where it combines with citrulline. That is a different organ and
      metabolic task. The question here asks for the principal substrate of the kidney's
      adaptive ammonia-producing pathway, which is glutamine.
  - q: Would changing the cause of metabolic acidosis change the renal substrate?
    a: >-
      Changing bicarbonate loss to lactic acidosis does not by itself change the principal
      renal ammoniagenesis substrate. Glutamine remains the substrate if the kidneys can
      mount the response. Renal impairment can limit that adaptation, and the causes require
      different clinical evaluation and treatment.

## What is this question asking?

**During metabolic acidosis, which amino acid do the kidneys primarily use to increase acid excretion?**

The original teaching case concerns bicarbonate loss through an ileostomy, with preserved kidney function
and increased urinary ammonium excretion. Its clinical background explains the acidosis. The final question
asks for the substrate used by the renal response, not the cause of the acidosis or a treatment.

## Follow two destinations

In the proximal tubule, glutamine supplies nitrogen for ammonium production. Subsequent nephron transport
supports its urinary excretion. Metabolism of the remaining carbon skeleton generates new bicarbonate
that returns to the blood. Keeping urine and blood separate explains why ammonia production alone is not
enough: its final destination matters. See [Weiner and Verlander](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23720285/).
