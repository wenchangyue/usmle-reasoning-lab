quick_answer: >-
  In **X-linked agammaglobulinemia (XLA)**, a defect in **Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK)** severely blocks B-cell maturation
  at the **pre-B** stage, so B cells are nearly absent and IgG, IgA and IgM are all low, while T cells are normal. Very few
  mature B cells reach the lymph node, so typical **lymphoid follicles** (the B-cell zone, outer cortex) don't form, and
  **germinal centers**—reactions inside follicles—don't form either. The **paracortex** (T-cell zone with HEVs), sinuses
  and lymphatic vessels remain. Rule: ask which cell is broken, then where that cell lives.
faq:
  - q: What is the difference between a lymphoid follicle and the paracortex?
    a: >-
      Follicles sit in the outer cortex under the subcapsular sinus and are the B-cell zone: B cells, follicular
      dendritic cells, and germinal centers with a mantle zone, where antibody responses mature. The paracortex sits
      deeper, between the follicles and the medulla, and is the T-cell zone: T cells, ordinary dendritic cells, and high
      endothelial venules (HEVs), where T cells meet antigen-carrying dendritic cells. Follicles expand in bacterial
      infection and some autoimmune disease; the paracortex expands in viral infection such as infectious mononucleosis.
  - q: Why do infants with XLA stay well for about six months?
    a: >-
      Maternal IgG crosses the placenta through the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) on the syncytiotrophoblast. IgG is the
      only class that crosses in meaningful amounts. That borrowed IgG is broken down over the first months; a healthy
      infant's own IgG rises to replace it, but in XLA nothing rises, so infections often start after about six months.
  - q: Why does XLA cause pneumococcal infections and Giardia?
    a: >-
      IgG opsonizes encapsulated bacteria such as Streptococcus pneumoniae and encapsulated strains of Haemophilus
      influenzae, activates complement and neutralizes toxins and viruses. Secretory IgA, carried across the gut lining
      by the polymeric immunoglobulin receptor, blocks attachment at mucosal surfaces; without it Giardia settles in the
      small intestine. Clearing enteroviruses also depends on neutralizing antibody, so live oral polio vaccine is avoided.
  - q: How do XLA, DiGeorge, SCID, CVID and X-linked hyper-IgM differ in the lymph node?
    a: >-
      XLA: no follicles or germinal centers, paracortex present. DiGeorge (22q11.2 deletion, underdeveloped thymus):
      paracortex underdeveloped to a variable degree, follicles can be present. SCID: T-cell function severely impaired,
      B-cell numbers depend on subtype, nodes very poorly developed. CVID: B cells usually present but late
      differentiation impaired; nodes can be hyperplastic. X-linked hyper-IgM (CD40 ligand deficiency): primary follicles
      present, germinal centers abortive, IgM normal or high.
  - q: How does the spleen map onto the lymph node?
    a: >-
      In the white pulp, the periarteriolar lymphoid sheath (PALS) is the T-cell zone, like the paracortex; splenic
      follicles are B-cell zones that can contain germinal centers; the marginal zone holds marginal-zone B cells and
      macrophages and catches encapsulated bacteria. In XLA splenic follicles are missing; in DiGeorge the PALS is
      underdeveloped to a variable degree.
