quick_answer: >-
  The answer is **fatty acid synthesis**. The deficient enzyme is glucose-6-phosphate
  dehydrogenase (**G6PD**), the rate-limiting first step of the pentose phosphate pathway. Its
  oxidative phase produces **NADPH**. NADPH has two jobs: **build** (reductive biosynthesis of
  fatty acids, cholesterol and steroids) and **protect** (keep glutathione reduced so red cells
  survive oxidant stress). The clinical story points to "protect", but glutathione is not among
  the options, so the question is testing "build". Rule: the enzyme names the pathway, the
  pathway names the molecule, and the options name the job.
faq:
  - q: Why is the answer fatty acid synthesis and not something about red cells?
    a: >-
      The question asks which process needs the molecule G6PD generates. That molecule is NADPH.
      Of the five options, only fatty acid synthesis consumes NADPH. Antioxidant defense through
      reduced glutathione also needs NADPH, but it is not offered, so the remaining NADPH job,
      reductive biosynthesis, is the answer.
  - q: How much NADPH does the pentose phosphate pathway make?
    a: >-
      The oxidative phase makes two NADPH for each glucose-6-phosphate: one at G6PD and one at
      6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase, with loss of one carbon as CO2, giving ribulose-5-phosphate.
  - q: Why do fava beans, sulfa drugs or infection trigger hemolysis in G6PD deficiency?
    a: >-
      Each of them raises oxidant stress in red cells. Without enough G6PD, red cells cannot make
      enough NADPH to keep glutathione reduced, so oxidized hemoglobin denatures into Heinz bodies.
      Splenic macrophages remove those clumps, leaving bite cells, and the damaged cells are
      destroyed. Typical triggers include fava beans, primaquine, dapsone, sulfa drugs,
      nitrofurantoin and, most commonly, infection.
  - q: Why is the patient usually a young man?
    a: >-
      G6PD deficiency is X-linked, so a male with one affected X chromosome is affected.
      Heterozygous females are often carriers, but some are affected because X-inactivation
      leaves a share of their red cells without working G6PD.
