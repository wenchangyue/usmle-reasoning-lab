faq:
  - q: Why can't this be ongoing cytokine release syndrome?
    a: >-
      Cytokine release syndrome is a real and common toxicity — in the pivotal pediatric and young-adult study it
      occurred in 77% of patients, 48% of whom received tocilizumab, an antibody against the interleukin-6 receptor.
      But it begins in the first days to roughly two weeks after infusion, and the stem says her episode resolved. A
      real toxicity quoted at the wrong point on the clock is bait. Ongoing cytokine release would also not produce
      low IgG, IgA and IgM with normal counts and a normal marrow.
  - q: Why can't autologous CAR T cells cause graft-versus-host disease?
    a: >-
      Graft-versus-host disease needs donor immune cells and a genetically different host — two genomes. Her T cells
      were collected from her own blood by apheresis, modified and given back, so the product is autologous: one
      person, one genome. The mechanism has no second genome to work with. Her quiet skin, gut and liver confirm it,
      but they are not the reason. Make the same product from a healthy donor — off-the-shelf, allogeneic — and
      graft-versus-host disease becomes a real consideration again.
  - q: How can her tetanus titer still be protective if she has no B cells?
    a: >-
      Plasma cells are not uniform in CD19: many express it, some do not. A CD19-negative, long-lived plasma-cell
      subset exists, and a receptor aimed at CD19 cannot reach it. In recipients of CD19-directed CAR T cells,
      antibody-secreting plasma cells were still present in the marrow of eight patients despite absent CD19+CD20+
      B cells, documented out to about 25 months; total immunoglobulin declined while several vaccine- and
      pathogen-specific titers stayed relatively stable. So some old antibody persists while little new antibody is
      added. What is clearly impaired is making new antibody against new antigens.
  - q: What is the difference between off-target and on-target, off-tumor?
    a: >-
      Off-target means the receptor binds something it was never designed to bind — a recognition failure. On-target,
      off-tumor means it binds exactly the right antigen, on a normal cell that also carries it: the recognition
      working correctly on the wrong cell. B-cell aplasia after CD19-directed CAR T cells is described in the
      literature as an expected on-target, off-tumor effect that leads to hypogammaglobulinemia. Note the word
      expected — it was derivable from the choice of target before anyone was treated.
  - q: Does immunoglobulin replacement fix the problem?
    a: >-
      It supplies the product; it does not repair the production line. Pooled human immunoglobulin will not bring back
      a B cell and will not make a plasma cell, and stopping the infusions returns the deficit. Monthly replacement is
      prioritized for patients with severe or recurrent bacterial infections. Best-practice recommendations exist;
      what is limited is high-quality prospective evidence, so practice varies between centers. In one retrospective
      cohort replacement was associated with a decreased risk of mortality — an association in a retrospective
      comparison, not a demonstrated survival benefit.
  - q: Why do returning B cells matter in B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia?
    a: >-
      Absent B cells support ongoing functional activity of the engineered cells; returning B cells may indicate
      reduced functional persistence. It is an indirect readout, weighed alongside recovery timing, direct testing for
      the product, and minimal residual disease. The original cohort reported product persistence of 68% (95% CI
      50–92) and relapse-free B-cell aplasia of 73% (95% CI 57–94) at six months — two probabilities in the same
      range, consistent with a link rather than proof that one tracks the other. In B-ALL the engineered cells are
      also what holds the leukemia down, so B-cell recovery is treated as a relapse-risk signal.
  - q: What changes if the target is BCMA instead of CD19?
    a: >-
      Run the same method again. BCMA (gene TNFRSF17) is expressed by normal and malignant plasma cells and a subset
      of mature B cells, so a BCMA-directed product empties a different box: the antibody factories themselves, rather
      than their precursors. The result is still an antibody deficit, but products aimed at different B-lineage
      antigens create different immune deficits and may need different infection-prevention strategies.
