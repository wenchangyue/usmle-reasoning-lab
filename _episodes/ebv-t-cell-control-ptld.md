quick_answer: >-
  EBV hides in B cells. B cells that switch on the EBV **growth program** (EBNA2, EBNA-LP, EBNA3A/3C, LMP1) display
  pieces of those latent proteins on **MHC class I**, and **EBV-specific cytotoxic (CD8+) T cells**, with CD4+ help,
  read and kill them. After an **allogeneic** transplant conditioned with **antithymocyte globulin (ATG)** and followed by
  **cyclosporine**, those T cells are removed and silenced, so infected B cells expand: **EBV-positive post-transplant
  lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD)**. Neutralizing antibodies against entry proteins such as gp350 act on free virus
  and cannot undo an infection already inside a B cell. Rule: ask who normally guards the virus, and who was removed.
faq:
  - q: Why don't neutralizing antibodies control EBV-infected B cells?
    a: >-
      Neutralizing antibodies against viral entry proteins such as gp350 coat free virus particles and block attachment
      to CD21 on B cells. Latent EBV sits inside the B-cell nucleus as an episome, so these antibodies cannot undo an
      established infection. Infected cells are found by T cells that read viral peptides on MHC class I. A positive
      anti-capsid IgG shows past infection; it does not measure neutralizing function. Rituximab is a different route:
      it binds CD20 on the B cell itself and removes the cell, virus and all.
  - q: Why does PTLD follow the transplant rather than chemotherapy alone?
    a: >-
      Conditioning (high-dose chemotherapy, sometimes radiation, sometimes anti-T-cell antibodies) causes the immune gap;
      the stem-cell infusion is the rescue. Neutrophils and NK cells recover early, while T cells recover slowly, and
      allogeneic recipients also receive T-cell-suppressing drugs to prevent rejection and GVHD. In a cohort of more than
      18,000 allogeneic marrow transplants, PTLD incidence peaked 1 to 5 months after transplant.
  - q: Is EBV PTLD common after autologous transplant?
    a: >-
      No. In a registry of more than 64,000 stem-cell transplants, the 2-year rate of EBV-positive PTLD was about 0.79%
      after allogeneic and 0.11% after autologous transplants. The main risk factors (ATG, T-cell depletion, unrelated or
      mismatched donors, cord blood, GVHD) are mainly allogeneic features. After autologous transplant, herpes zoster
      remains a common complication, whereas EBV-positive PTLD is rare.
  - q: What is the difference between reactivation, primary infection and PTLD growth?
    a: >-
      A primary infection is the first infection with a virus and needs an outside source. Reactivation is a latent
      virus switching back to the lytic cycle and making new particles. PTLD growth is infected B cells running the
      growth program and dividing; it does not require new virus particles. In solid-organ transplants an
      EBV-seronegative recipient is a key risk, because the first infection meets suppressed T cells.
  - q: How is EBV PTLD monitored and treated?
    a: >-
      High-risk patients have blood EBV DNA tracked by quantitative PCR; a rising load prompts evaluation, and tissue
      establishes the diagnosis (EBER-positive B-cell proliferation). The ECIL-6 guideline (2016) lists rituximab,
      reduction of immunosuppression when feasible, and EBV-specific cytotoxic T cells as first-line options, and
      discourages antiviral drugs. In long-term follow-up of EBV-specific T-cell infusions, none of 101 prophylaxis
      recipients developed EBV lymphoproliferative disease and 11 of 13 treated patients had sustained remission.
