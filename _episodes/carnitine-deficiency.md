quick_answer: >-
  The substance that cannot be synthesised is **acetoacetate**, a ketone body. Blocking the
  carnitine shuttle stops long-chain fatty acids from entering mitochondria, so no fat-derived
  **acetyl-CoA** is made and the liver cannot produce ketones — hence low glucose with
  **negative** urine ketones (hypoketotic hypoglycaemia). The same acetyl-CoA shortage also fails
  to activate **pyruvate carboxylase** (gluconeogenesis stalls) and fails to make
  **N-acetylglutamate**, the activator of **CPS-1** (ammonia can rise). The disease is
  **systemic primary carnitine deficiency**: the **SLC22A5** gene builds a defective **OCTN2**
  transporter, so the kidney wastes carnitine (high urinary, low plasma) and heart and muscle
  cannot take it up. Treatment is lifelong oral **L-carnitine**.
faq:
  - q: Why is the answer acetoacetate and not palmitate?
    a: >-
      Acetoacetate is a ketone body, and ketones are made from acetyl-CoA generated by burning fat.
      With the carnitine shuttle blocked, that acetyl-CoA never appears, so ketone synthesis fails —
      which is exactly why the urine ketones are negative while the glucose is low. Palmitate is the
      opposite direction: it is a fatty acid the body *builds* when energy is plentiful. Fatty acid
      synthesis is untouched in this disease. The word "fatty acid" is the bait; the direction is wrong.
  - q: Which gene and which protein are broken?
    a: >-
      The gene is SLC22A5 and the protein it encodes is OCTN2, a sodium-dependent carnitine
      transporter in the plasma membrane. OCTN2 is not an enzyme — it is a doorway whose only job is to
      carry carnitine from outside the cell to inside. Inheritance is autosomal recessive, so both
      copies must be affected. Most patients retain partial transport rather than none, which matters
      for treatment.
  - q: Where does carnitine come from, and why can't the patient just eat more?
    a: >-
      Roughly three quarters of body carnitine comes from food (mostly meat and dairy) and about a
      quarter is synthesised from lysine and methionine, in a pathway that requires vitamin C. The
      catch is that the final synthetic enzyme, BBOX, is present in liver, kidney and brain but not in
      cardiac or skeletal muscle. Those two tissues hold almost all of the body's carnitine and cannot
      make a single molecule of it — they are entirely dependent on uptake through OCTN2. Eating more
      does not rescue them, because the kidney is simultaneously wasting carnitine into the urine and
      the door into the muscle barely opens.
  - q: Why does urinary carnitine go UP while blood carnitine goes down?
    a: >-
      OCTN2 also performs renal tubular reabsorption of filtered carnitine. When it fails, carnitine
      that has been filtered is lost in the urine instead of being reclaimed. So the pairing of high
      urinary carnitine with low plasma carnitine points to a renal leak, and is the biochemical
      fingerprint of the disease.
  - q: If the transporter is broken, how can taking L-carnitine possibly help?
    a: >-
      Four reasons stack up. Most mutations leave a narrow door rather than no door, so some transport
      remains. Raising the blood concentration far above normal increases the absolute flux through
      that narrow door (mass action). Critically, the amount of carnitine needed to keep the shuttle
      running is far lower than the amount normally held in tissue storage, so tissue levels can stay
      below normal while cardiac function recovers — the goal is normal flux, not normal levels. And
      because the renal leak cannot be plugged, treatment is lifelong and daily. Reported cases show
      cardiomyopathy and even a shortened QT interval improving after oral carnitine.
  - q: If fat is accumulating, why isn't the child obese?
    a: >-
      Adipose tissue is not the broken part — it stores and releases fat normally. The block is
      downstream, inside mitochondria, so the child cannot reach the fuel he already has. That is a
      chronic energy deficit, which produces poor growth and late motor milestones rather than obesity.
      The fat that does accumulate is ectopic and microscopic: droplets inside muscle fibres (a lipid
      myopathy on biopsy) and fat inside hepatocytes.
  - q: Why do the heart and skeletal muscle fail, and why does walking start out normal?
    a: >-
      At rest the heart derives most of its energy from fatty acid oxidation, making it the most
      fat-dependent organ; chronic ATP shortfall weakens the pump, the chamber dilates, and you hear an
      S3 gallop with a murmur from the stretched valve ring. Skeletal muscle can cover the first short
      burst of walking with glycogen, creatine phosphate and blood glucose, but sustained work requires
      switching to fat — and that switch fails, so the child falls off a cliff rather than tiring
      gradually. Unburned long-chain lipid also damages the fibres, which raises CK.
  - q: How is this different from CPT-2 deficiency and MCAD deficiency?
    a: >-
      In CPT-2 deficiency the machinery behind the door is broken while carnitine itself is normal or
      high; it typically presents in older patients with exercise-induced muscle pain and myoglobinuria,
      and giving carnitine does not correct it because carnitine was never what was missing. In MCAD
      deficiency there is hypoketotic hypoglycaemia too, but medium-chain fatty acids bypass the
      carnitine shuttle entirely, so the heart is typically spared and carnitine is only mildly reduced.
      The state of the heart is a useful clue to chain length.
