quick_answer: >-
  The mutated receptor is a **membrane-bound receptor coupled with a G protein**. The disease is
  **familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia (FHH)**; the gene is ***CASR***; the protein is the
  **calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR)**, a **class C GPCR** — a large extracellular Venus-flytrap
  domain that binds Ca²⁺ itself, a cysteine-rich linker, **seven transmembrane passes**, a
  disulfide-linked homodimer, coupling to **Gq/11** and **Gi/o**. It sits on parathyroid chief
  cells **and** on the **basolateral** membrane of the renal thick ascending limb, so both organs
  read the same blood calcium. Lose function and the gland fails to suppress PTH while the kidney
  independently fails to excrete calcium — which is why the **urine** calcium, not the PTH,
  separates FHH from primary hyperparathyroidism.
faq:
  - q: What is the difference between CASR and CaSR?
    a: >-
      They are the same four letters and the same spoken word; the difference exists only on the
      page. ***CASR*** in italic capitals is the **gene**, on the long arm of chromosome 3. **CaSR**
      with a lower-case "a" is the **protein** the gene builds — the calcium-sensing receptor. If
      you are listening rather than reading you cannot tell them apart, so use the sentence instead
      of the spelling: a gene is a set of instructions, a protein is the thing built from them. If
      it is sitting in a membrane doing a job, it is the protein.
  - q: What is the receptor's ligand — a hormone?
    a: >-
      No. The ligand is a **bare calcium ion**. Most receptors wait for a molecule another cell made
      and released on purpose; this one measures a concentration directly. That is what lets it act
      as a thermostat for a mineral rather than a mailbox for a message.
  - q: Why does activating this receptor make the parathyroid do LESS?
    a: >-
      Because it is a brake, not an accelerator. High calcium closes the flytrap, the G protein
      fires, and **PTH secretion is inhibited**. This runs against the instinct that "receptor
      activated" means "cell switched on," and it is a recurring exam trap. Break the sensor and the
      gland never feels the brake at a calcium where it should — which is why the PTH is
      inappropriately un-suppressed rather than dramatically high.
  - q: Which membrane of the kidney tubule is the receptor on, and why does that matter?
    a: >-
      The **basolateral** membrane of the thick ascending limb — the blood side, not the side facing
      the urine. That is the whole derivation of this case. Both the gland's sensor and the kidney's
      sensor face the blood, so both read the same number. One broken gene therefore causes a gland
      problem and a kidney problem **side by side**, with neither downstream of the other.
  - q: How does the kidney part actually work?
    a: >-
      About a quarter of filtered calcium is reabsorbed in the thick ascending limb, and entirely
      **between** the cells rather than through them. NKCC2 pulls in Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻, potassium leaks
      back to the lumen through ROMK and chloride exits basolaterally, leaving the lumen
      electrically **positive** — and that voltage is what pushes Ca²⁺ back through a paracellular
      pore made of claudin-16 and claudin-19. When blood calcium is high, CaSR lowers miR-9 and
      miR-374, which raises **claudin-14**, and claudin-14 physically blocks that pore so calcium
      leaves in the urine. In FHH that induction is **blunted**, so reabsorption stays higher than
      the calcium level warrants.
  - q: Why is the hypercalcemia only mild? Both organs are pushing calcium up.
    a: >-
      Because the set point moved rather than the feedback disappearing. The mutant receptor still
      detects calcium; it just needs more of it, so the system re-stabilises at a higher number and
      holds there. Be careful with the usual contrast, though: primary hyperparathyroidism **also**
      involves a shifted set point and reduced CaSR expression, and normohormonal hyperparathyroidism
      exists — so "is the feedback intact?" does not separate them. What separates them is **where
      the defect lives**: FHH is germline and systemic, an adenoma is a local clone.
  - q: Where does the extra calcium actually go?
    a: >-
      The question assumes an ongoing surplus that has to be stored somewhere, and there isn't one.
      Total body calcium is about 1000 g with over 99 % in bone, while the entire extracellular
      fluid holds roughly **1 g**. Running about 15 % above baseline means the extracellular
      compartment holds around **150 mg** more — smaller than a single day's net calcium absorption
      (~200 mg), and about 0.015 % of the skeleton. It is a one-time step up in a level, not a daily
      flow. A static difference between two blood concentrations cannot tell you which door that
      calcium came through, and long-term balance always involves kidney, gut and bone together.
  - q: Does that mean bone stops releasing calcium?
    a: >-
      No — bone is resorbed and rebuilt every day of your life. What is small in FHH is the **net**
      movement, not the traffic. In this patient, at 16, the net still points *into* bone because she
      is finishing a skeleton. Clinically it shows up as fracture incidence in FHH type 1 **not**
      being increased — though fractures and osteoporosis can still occur, so it is not a promise of
      perfect bone.
  - q: What happens with two bad copies?
    a: >-
      **Neonatal severe hyperparathyroidism**, usually within the first six months and often in the
      first days to weeks: severe hypercalcemia, markedly raised PTH, dehydration from polyuria,
      hypotonia, failure to thrive, and bone changes with fractures — including ribs, which is what
      makes it life-threatening rather than merely severe. Untreated it carries a high mortality in
      infancy. Note the reservoir argument runs the opposite way to intuition: a newborn's skeleton
      holds around **30 g**, not the adult's 1000 g. The driver is unopposed, continuous PTH with no
      stop signal — because the detector itself is broken, no calcium level tells the gland to ease
      off. Reservoir size decides how fast the damage shows, not whether it happens.
  - q: Can those children reach adulthood?
    a: >-
      With treatment, yes. Acutely: fluids and bisphosphonates. **Cinacalcet**, a calcimimetic, is
      used to avoid or delay surgery — but the evidence is case reports and small series, it is an
      off-label use, and the response is **genotype-dependent**, with reports of both success and
      outright failure. When it fails, surgery is the treatment, and the outcome depends on which
      operation: a subtotal resection may leave enough function, whereas total parathyroidectomy
      means lifelong calcium and active vitamin D.
  - q: How reliable is the calcium-to-creatinine clearance ratio?
    a: >-
      Good enough to build an exam question on, and weaker than most people assume. A ratio below
      **0.01** captures only about **65 %** of FHH type 1, and roughly **18 %** of surgically
      confirmed primary hyperparathyroidism also sits below it. A ratio at or below 0.02 is what
      should trigger genetic testing — and more than a quarter of people with a convincing clinical
      diagnosis of FHH have **no** identifiable variant in *CASR*, *GNA11* or *AP2S1*, so a negative
      gene test does not clear them either.
  - q: Why doesn't parathyroidectomy cure FHH?
    a: >-
      Because the defect is not in the glands. It is in the sensor, and the sensor is in every
      remaining gland and in the kidney too. Surgery does not routinely cure it, which is why real
      FHH families so often contain a relative who had a neck operation that never worked. That is
      the cost of missing this diagnosis: an operation on a well person, for a problem the operation
      was never aimed at.
  - q: How is this different from MEN2A?
    a: >-
      MEN2A is caused by *RET*, a **receptor tyrosine kinase**, and it does include primary
      hyperparathyroidism in roughly a quarter of patients — so it survives the persistent
      hypercalcemia, the family history and the un-suppressed PTH. It is outweighed, not eliminated,
      by the urine calcium: MEN2A hyperparathyroidism is genuine hyperparathyroidism, so those
      patients typically put calcium **into** the urine. Carriers can also be asymptomatic for years,
      so "they look well" does not rule it out on its own.

## The case

A 16-year-old girl has routine laboratory studies drawn before elective arthroscopic repair of a
torn meniscus. She feels well. Serum calcium is **11.1 mg/dL** (reference 8.6–10.2). Three weeks
later she is still asymptomatic with a normal examination, and the calcium is **11.0**. Her father
and a paternal aunt were each told years ago that their calcium was "a little high," and neither was
given an explanation.

| Test | Value | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Intact PTH | **68 pg/mL** | 15–65 |
| 24-hour urinary calcium | **42 mg/24 h** | 100–250 |
| Calcium-to-creatinine clearance ratio | **0.006** | — |
| Serum magnesium | **2.3 mg/dL** | 1.7–2.2 |
| 25-hydroxyvitamin D | **34 ng/mL** | 30–100 |
| Phosphorus · creatinine | normal | — |

> A mutation in which of the following receptors is most likely responsible for this patient's
> laboratory abnormalities?
>
> **A.** Intracellular receptor with a DNA-binding domain
> **B.** Membrane-bound receptor coupled with a G protein
> **C.** Transmembrane ligand-gated ion channel
> **D.** Transmembrane receptor with intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity
> **E.** Transmembrane receptor activating the JAK/STAT pathway

**Answer: B.**

*All laboratory values above were constructed for teaching and chosen to be internally consistent
with the published FHH biochemical profile. They are not from a real patient and not from any
question bank; the vignette is an original construction.*

## The one rule to carry out of this

High calcium with a PTH that isn't suppressed — the next value you look at is the **urine** calcium.

- **LOW** urine calcium points to a broken **sensor** rather than a broken gland: FHH, *CASR*, a
  G protein-coupled receptor, and most of those patients are best left alone.
- **HIGH** urine calcium points to an autonomous **gland**: hyperparathyroidism, and that is the one
  that goes to surgery.

Points to — not proves. The two overlap more than any flowchart admits, and the numbers above say by
how much.

And the structural half: when a question asks for a receptor **class**, stop trying to recall a table
of diseases and ask what the receptor physically *is*. Seven passes through the membrane, a flytrap
on the outside closed around a bare calcium ion, a G protein docked on the inside. That is option B,
described part by part, and it was available before you knew anything about the disease.
